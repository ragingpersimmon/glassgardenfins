10 Aquarium Questions I Keep Coming Back To

Answers to the fishkeeping questions that come up again and again.

Aquarium guide August 21, 2026

Some questions never really go away in this hobby. New keepers ask them in forums, experienced keepers still second-guess themselves over them, and honestly, most of us have gotten the answer wrong at least once before it stuck. Here are ten I hear constantly, answered the way I wish someone had laid them out for me early on.

1. How Much Fish Food Is the Right Amount?

Overfeeding is probably the single easiest mistake to make in this hobby, and almost everyone makes it at some point. Part of the problem is that fish are convincing actors — they’ll swarm the surface looking half-starved five minutes after already eating plenty. Believe the amount they’ve eaten, not the performance they put on afterward.

A good rule of thumb: feed only what your fish can finish in about a minute or two. Not what would make them stop begging — what they can actually clear. Drop in a small amount, watch it disappear (or not), and adjust from there. If flakes are still drifting down to the substrate a few minutes later, you went in too heavy.

It’s worth remembering that a lot of species are opportunistic feeders by nature. In the wild, food shows up unpredictably, so eating whenever it’s available is just smart survival — not evidence of an empty stomach. That eager rush to the glass when you walk by usually means “I’ve learned this is when food happens,” not “I’m starving.” If you want a real read on whether your fish are getting enough, look at body condition instead — a fish that’s neither pinched-in nor ballooning is telling you more than its feeding-time enthusiasm ever will.

Most adult community fish do fine on one or two small feedings a day, though juveniles need more frequent meals since they’re putting on growth fast. Some keepers even skip a day here and there for adults, and the fish are none the worse for it.

Bottom feeders complicate things a bit — corydoras, loaches, and plecos aren’t always great at competing with faster surface fish, so the fix isn’t more food overall, it’s food that actually reaches them (sinking pellets, timed for when the water column’s less chaotic). Shrimp are their own case entirely: in an established tank they’re mostly grazing biofilm and algae around the clock, so they often need barely any supplemental feeding, and overfeeding them tends to just rot in the substrate.

2. How Much Sun Do Fish Need?

None, really — at least not in the way people assume. Sticking a tank in direct sun doesn’t do your fish any favors, and it tends to cause more problems than it solves.

What actually matters is a consistent light-dark cycle, not sunlight specifically. Fish rest and settle into rhythm during darkness the same way most animals do, so a tank that’s lit 24/7 is doing them no favors. Somewhere in the 6–10 hour range per day works for most freshwater setups, with the exact number shifting depending on what you’re growing and how the tank’s balanced overall.

The trouble with a sunny window is that it’s basically impossible to control. Light intensity swings wildly through the day, algae loves the extra energy, and depending on your climate, temperature can start swinging too. You can run a perfectly reasonable lighting schedule on your fixture and still lose the battle if a few hours of afternoon sun are hitting the glass every day.

Fish don’t need UV exposure just because they’re fish — that’s a holdover from reptile-keeping logic that doesn’t really apply here. A handful of specialized species have unusual light requirements, but that’s driven by their specific biology, not some universal rule.

Planted tanks add a wrinkle because plants genuinely do need light to photosynthesize — but more light isn’t automatically better. If your plants can’t use the extra light efficiently (because nutrients or carbon aren’t keeping pace), you’re just handing algae a bigger energy budget. A reasonable starting point is 7–8 hours a day on a timer, adjusted over a few weeks based on how plants and algae respond. And skip the manual on/off switching — a cheap timer will do more for consistency than remembering to flip a switch every evening.

3. What Happened to My Shrimp’s Skin?

You spot something on the substrate that looks exactly like a dead, translucent shrimp — legs, antennae, the whole shape — and your stomach drops. Good news: it’s very likely a molt, not a body.

Shrimp wear their skeleton on the outside, and unlike your bones, an exoskeleton can’t just stretch as the animal grows. So periodically, a shrimp grows a new, softer shell underneath the old one, then wriggles free and leaves the old shell behind. That discarded shell — called an exuvia — is what you’re looking at, and it can be shockingly convincing.

The tell is transparency and structure without substance. A molt looks hollow, papery, almost see-through, while an actual dead shrimp keeps its color and body. If you’re not sure, hold off on removing it — there’s a decent chance the shrimp (or its tankmates) will eat it themselves within a day or two, recycling minerals the shell contains. Plenty of experienced keepers leave healthy molts in the tank on purpose for exactly this reason.

Right after molting, a shrimp is at its most vulnerable — soft-shelled and often hiding until things harden up again. Don’t be surprised if you don’t see it for a while afterward.

When molts go wrong, it’s usually chemistry-related. General hardness matters a lot here since calcium and other minerals are what the new shell is built from, and low or unstable GH is a common culprit behind failed molts. Sudden parameter swings, poor acclimation, stress, and general health all play a role too. If you notice a pale ring forming around a shrimp’s body before it molts, that’s typically just the split point between old and new shell — not something to panic over on its own.

A single successful molt is a good sign, honestly — it means growth. What should actually worry you is a pattern: several shrimp dying or struggling with molts around the same time. That’s when it’s worth testing your water and looking hard at recent changes.

4. Why Is My Fish Staying at the Bottom of the Tank?

Depends entirely on who’s doing it. A corydoras working the substrate is just being a corydoras. A pleco glued to the glass near the bottom is having a completely normal day. It’s the fish that’s usually mid-water and suddenly isn’t that deserves attention.

Start with water quality — ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH, temperature. Ammonia and nitrite especially can be dangerous even in water that looks totally clear, so don’t trust your eyes here. Temperature deserves its own check too, since fish can’t regulate their body heat internally the way we do. Too cold and everything slows down; too warm and oxygen availability drops just as metabolic demand rises. Match the actual number against what the species needs.

If it’s a new fish, give it some slack — transport and a new environment are legitimately stressful, and hiding low in the tank for a while is a normal adjustment period, not a red flag on its own.

Where it gets more serious is when bottom-sitting shows up alongside other symptoms: rapid gill movement, clamped fins, refusing food, unusual spots, pale color, erratic swimming, or pulling away from normally social tankmates. None of those alone confirms a specific problem, but stack a few together and it’s worth investigating properly — starting with water and equipment, not the medicine cabinet. Reaching for treatment before ruling out an environmental cause is a common, understandable, and usually unhelpful first move.

5. Why Is My Fish Swimming at the Top of the Tank?

The question to ask yourself is whether the fish is hanging out up there or gasping up there — those are two very different situations.

Plenty of species are naturally surface-oriented and spend real time up top feeding, exploring, or holding territory. If it’s active, eating, coloring up normally, that’s probably just its personality. What changes the picture is rapid, repeated surface gulping — especially if several fish start doing it at once.

Oxygen enters water mainly at the surface, so anything that reduces surface agitation cuts down on gas exchange. Warm water also holds less dissolved oxygen to begin with, which is why a warm, heavily stocked tank with a flat, still surface can run into real trouble. Check your filter first — reduced flow, a clogged intake, or a struggling impeller can tank your oxygenation fast, and it’s an easy thing to overlook when everything looks fine from across the room. (Just don’t nuke all your bio-media in the process — that bacteria is doing real work even if it looks grungy.)

Rule out ammonia and nitrite too, since clear water tells you nothing about what’s dissolved in it. And if you’re running injected CO₂ on a planted tank, that deserves particular suspicion — too much CO₂ interferes with gas exchange and can send fish straight to the surface gasping, which is an equipment problem to fix, not an oxygen shortage to compensate for.

If several fish start gasping at once, don’t wait around — check oxygenation, temperature, and equipment immediately, and consider adding an air stone or boosting surface movement while you sort out the underlying cause.

6. How Often Should You Change Aquarium Water?

There’s no single correct percentage, and anyone who tells you there is hasn’t kept very many different kinds of tanks. A crowded community tank, a lightly stocked planted tank, and a mature shrimp colony are going to want fairly different maintenance rhythms. The better question is what’s actually accumulating in your water between changes.

Filtration handles a lot, but it doesn’t make everything disappear — fish waste, leftover food, and ongoing biological processes all generate compounds that just build up until something removes them. A water change does that removal job, while also topping up minerals and diluting whatever’s accumulated.

20–30% weekly is a fine place to start for a typical community tank, but treat it as a starting point, not gospel. If nitrate is climbing fast week over week, that’s a signal worth chasing down — stocking, feeding habits, plant load, filtration — rather than just doing bigger and bigger changes to compensate. Fix the source, not just the symptom.

Shrimp complicate the math because they’re genuinely sensitive to sudden parameter shifts. Smaller, more frequent changes tend to sit better with a shrimp colony than occasional big ones, and matching your replacement water reasonably closely on temperature, GH, KH, and pH matters more here than almost anywhere else in the hobby.

As for substrate vacuuming — depends on the setup. Heavily planted tanks generally don’t need every inch disturbed, while bare-bottom or lightly planted tanks benefit from regular debris removal. And no, water changes don’t strip out your beneficial bacteria — that colonizes filter media, substrate, and hardscape, not the water itself, so you’re not resetting your cycle every time you top off the tank.

Even a tank testing perfectly clean still benefits from routine changes. Consistency, done regularly and predictably, beats waiting for a problem and then overcorrecting.

7. Why Is My Aquarium Water Cloudy?

Cloudy water is alarming mostly because it happens fast and looks dramatic — but it’s rarely the emergency it appears to be. The real question is what kind of cloudy you’re dealing with.

A white or grayish haze is usually a bacterial bloom, and it’s extremely common in newer tanks as the microbial community establishes itself. It can look genuinely bad — like fog rolling through the water — while actually being a completely normal part of the tank maturing. These blooms typically clear up within a week as things settle, as long as ammonia and nitrite stay in check in the meantime.

Green water is a different animal entirely — free-floating algae suspended through the whole water column rather than growing on surfaces, usually driven by a combination of light and available nutrients. Brown or tea-colored water often traces back to new driftwood leaching tannins, while a dusty brown tint can just be fine substrate particles that got kicked up and haven’t settled yet.

The instinct when things go cloudy is to tear the whole tank apart — scrub everything, replace media, do a massive water change. Resist that. It can destabilize things further and doesn’t actually fix whatever caused the cloudiness in the first place. Better to work backward: did you just start the tank, add substrate or driftwood, deep-clean the filter, bump up feeding, or add a bunch of new fish? The cause is usually sitting right there once you think it through.

Test the water regardless of what the cloudiness looks like — appearance alone won’t tell you if ammonia or nitrite is actually present. And if cloudy water shows up alongside fish gasping or acting lethargic, that combination is worth acting on immediately rather than waiting it out.

8. How Do I Know If My Aquarium Filter Is Big Enough?

GPH ratings on filter boxes are useful but overrated. Real-world flow ends up lower than advertised once you factor in media, tubing, and buildup over time, and turnover rate (how many times the filter theoretically cycles your tank’s volume per hour) is more of a rough comparison tool than a literal measurement.

Bigger isn’t automatically better, either. Cranking up flow can genuinely stress shrimp, small fish, and long-finned species that prefer calmer water — the goal is adequate filtration paired with flow your specific livestock can handle comfortably, not maximum horsepower for its own sake.

Biological capacity matters more than raw flow in most cases. A filter with plenty of surface area for beneficial bacteria is doing the heavy lifting on water quality even if its flow rating isn’t flashy. Mechanical filtration is the other half of the equation — fine floss for crystal-clear water, coarser media for bigger debris without clogging as fast — and either way, it needs regular attention so it doesn’t just become a clog waiting to happen.

Signs your filter’s undersized: persistent debris, weak circulation, media that clogs fast, water quality problems despite reasonable upkeep. But be careful with that last one — overfeeding and overstocking can overwhelm even a genuinely large filter, so don’t assume undersized before ruling out husbandry. On the flip side, if fish are constantly fighting the current or avoiding whole sections of the tank, flow is probably too aggressive and needs diffusing with a spray bar or baffle.

The best filter is the one matched to your actual stocking and inhabitants, not the one with the biggest number on the box.

9. How Many Fish Can I Put in My Aquarium?

The old one-inch-per-gallon rule needs to die. A one-inch fish and a six-inch fish don’t produce comparable waste, need comparable space, or behave anything alike, so treating them as interchangeable units never made much sense.

Adult size trips people up constantly — that adorable inch-long fish at the store might triple or quadruple in size, and stocking decisions should be built around what it becomes, not what it looks like in the bag. Social needs matter just as much: tetras, rasboras, and corydoras are genuinely happier and better-behaved in proper groups (six to eight for most schoolers, six or more for corydoras) than kept in twos and threes. Sometimes the right call is fewer species in bigger, healthier groups rather than a little bit of everything.

Territory is its own factor, separate from bioload entirely — two low-waste fish can still clash constantly if they both want the same six inches of real estate, and some species are territorial regardless of tank size. Hardscape and plants help by breaking up sightlines and giving fish somewhere to disappear to.

“My filter can handle it” is a trap, too — filtration processes waste, but it doesn’t create swimming room, defuse territorial disputes, or change how big a fish eventually gets. Every animal you add is additional biological demand on the system, and adding livestock gradually gives that system time to catch up rather than getting overwhelmed all at once.

A tank that looks packed and impressive can quietly be a much harder tank to keep stable than one that looks almost sparse. The better question isn’t how many fish fit — it’s what community the tank can actually support long-term, comfortably, for everyone in it.

10. Why Are My Aquarium Plants Turning Brown?

Brown doesn’t automatically mean dying, and that distinction matters more than almost anything else here.

Plants shed older leaves adjusting to a new tank all the time, especially if they were grown emersed (above water) at a nursery and are now adapting to life fully submerged — old growth dies back while new growth pushes out, and the transition can look genuinely rough even when it’s completely normal. Cryptocoryne are famous for this — full-on “melting” after a move, then quietly regrowing from the roots given a few stable weeks.

New growth is your best diagnostic tool, honestly. If old leaves are struggling but fresh growth keeps coming in healthy, the plant’s adapting fine. If both old and new growth are deteriorating together, that’s when something’s actually wrong.

Light, nutrients, and carbon all interact rather than working independently, which is why “just add more light” is rarely the fix people hope it’ll be — insufficient light stunts growth, but excess light without matching nutrients and carbon just fuels algae instead. Nutrient deficiencies (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, iron, and various micronutrients) can produce pale, yellowing, or transparent leaves, though pinning down exactly which nutrient from appearance alone is genuinely tricky since several deficiencies look similar. Low-tech setups without injected CO₂ can absolutely grow healthy plants with the right species selection — it’s not a requirement, just an option for more demanding growers.

Give melting plants some room before yanking them out — as long as roots and growing points look alive, patience usually pays off. None of this is complicated once you know what to look for. The hard part is usually just resisting the urge to overreact before you’ve actually tested the water or thought it through.
